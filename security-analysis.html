Security Analysis: The Classic 1934 Edition

Benjamin Graham & David L. Dodd

A 30-topic study guide based on major ideas from the 1934 classic by Benjamin Graham and David Dodd, covering security analysis, bonds, preferred stocks, common-stock valuation, earnings, and asset values.

Important: These are original educational study notes created by ICT Forex Academy. They are not the complete book or an official chapter-by-chapter reproduction from the publisher. The 30 topics are learning sections inspired by the seven major parts and key analytical themes of the original 1934 edition; they are not the book's exact chapter titles or sequence. For a deeper understanding, read the original edition.

    Key concept

    The Purpose of Security Analysis

    The objective of security analysis is to understand the underlying business or claim and develop a reasoned estimate of value relative to price.

    • View a share as an ownership claim in a business, not merely as a ticker symbol.
    • The contractual rights of bonds and preferred securities differ from those of common equity.
    • Analysis helps clarify uncertainty; it does not guarantee future prices.
    Key concept

    Reading Financial Statements Together

    The balance sheet, income statement, and cash flow statement work together to show a company's financial position.

    • Alongside revenue growth, examine margins, working capital, and cash conversion.
    • Understand debt maturities, interest coverage, and off-balance-sheet commitments.
    • Accounting profit can differ from actual operating cash flow.
    Key concept

    Earning Power and Business Quality

    It can be misleading to treat an unusually profitable year as evidence of sustainable earnings power.

    • Review multi-year earnings history in the context of the business cycle.
    • Identify one-off gains, asset sales, and exceptional expenses.
    • Assess industry structure, competition, and customer concentration.
    Key concept

    Bonds and Preferred Securities

    When evaluating fixed-income claims, the issuer's ability to repay principal and the claim's contractual priority are central.

    • Assess the issuer's ability to pay interest and principal.
    • Understand covenants, seniority, collateral, maturity, and refinancing risk.
    • A higher yield can often signal higher credit risk.
    Key concept

    Common stock valuation

    Equity valuation depends on estimates of future earning capacity, assets, liabilities, and business risks.

    • Dividend history is useful, but also assess dividend sustainability and free cash flow.
    • Do not automatically equate book value with market value; intangible assets and liabilities matter.
    • Compare valuation multiples in the context of growth, profitability, leverage, and cyclicality.
    Key concept

    Margin of Safety and Conclusion

    Using conservative assumptions and maintaining a buffer in the purchase price are important disciplines in analysis.

    • Build base, bear, and bull scenarios rather than relying on an optimistic forecast.
    • If key information is unreliable, choosing not to invest is also a valid decision.
    • Without diversification and appropriate position sizing, even sound analysis may not protect a portfolio from risk.
    Study topic 7 of 30

    Scope and Limitations of Analysis

    Security analysis evaluates available facts to develop a reasoned estimate of a security's value; it does not provide certainty or precise market timing.

    • Evaluate quantitative facts such as earnings, assets, and debt alongside qualitative factors such as management and industry conditions.
    • Information may be incomplete, outdated, or misleading.
    • Document analytical conclusions together with their assumptions and supporting evidence.
    Study topic 8 of 30

    The Concept of Intrinsic Value

    Intrinsic value is an estimate derived from a business's earning capacity, assets, financial position, and the rights attached to a security.

    • Market price and estimated value may differ.
    • Treat value as a reasonable range rather than a single exact number.
    • An estimate is useful only when its assumptions are conservative and transparent.
    Study topic 9 of 30

    Quantitative and Qualitative Factors

    The Graham-Dodd approach analyses both financial figures and business realities.

    • Quantitative factors: earnings, assets, liabilities, cash flow, and coverage ratios.
    • Qualitative factors: management, competition, industry structure, and business stability.
    • A compelling business story does not automatically compensate for a weak balance sheet.
    Study topic 10 of 30

    Sources of information

    An analyst should verify facts using independent information sources in addition to company accounts.

    • Review annual reports, audited statements, bond indentures, and official filings.
    • Cross-check information against industry data, competitors, and historical records.
    • Do not accept management claims as facts without independent evidence.
    Study topic 11 of 30

    The Difference Between Investment and Speculation

    The book distinguishes investment through the standards of careful analysis, safety of principal, and an adequate return.

    • Do not present a speculative outcome as a safe investment.
    • Understand the legal rights attached to a security and its capacity to withstand downside risk.
    • Losses remain possible even after rigorous analysis.
    Study topic 12 of 30

    Classification of Securities

    Different securities have different legal claims and risk-return profiles.

    • Do not treat bonds, preferred stocks, and common stocks as equivalent.
    • Review payment priority, voting rights, collateral, and maturity terms.
    • The security's name is less important than its contractual terms and the issuer's ability to meet its obligations.
    Study topic 13 of 30

    Fixed-value investments: safety first

    The central test for fixed-income securities is the issuer's ability to pay promised interest and principal on time.

    • Assess the issuer's earning power and cash resources.
    • Examine the debt burden, maturity schedule, and refinancing risk.
    • A high coupon is not, by itself, proof of safety.
    Study topic 14 of 30

    Standards for Bond Investment

    Bond analysis should consider income coverage, asset protection, and issue terms together.

    • Calculate interest coverage using normalised earnings.
    • Understand the difference between secured and unsecured debt claims.
    • Covenants and seniority are important in the event of default.
    Study topic 15 of 30

    Railroad and Public Utility Bonds

    The 1934 edition discusses industry-specific bond analysis for railroads and utilities, whose cash flows and assets have distinct characteristics.

    • Understand regulation, capital expenditure, and operating stability.
    • Do not substitute generic ratios for industry-specific risk analysis.
    • Do not apply historical sector examples directly to today's markets.
    Study topic 16 of 30

    Special factors in bond analysis

    Bondholders must analyse the issuer's financial structure and competing claims.

    • Compare total debt and fixed charges with earning power.
    • Examine subsidiary guarantees and intercompany obligations.
    • Test how resilient interest payments would be during periods of weak earnings.
    Study topic 17 of 30

    The Structure of Preferred Stock

    Preferred stock is equity, but it may have dividend and liquidation priority over common stock; terms vary by issue.

    • Understand whether dividends are cumulative or non-cumulative.
    • Review voting rights, redemption terms, and conversion clauses.
    • Preferred dividends are not guaranteed payments like bond interest.
    Study topic 18 of 30

    Selecting Preferred Stocks

    Dividend coverage and the issuer's overall financial strength are important when selecting preferred securities.

    • Assess dividend obligations in the context of senior debt.
    • Examine asset backing and protective provisions.
    • Compare market yield with credit and liquidity risks.
    Study topic 19 of 30

    Income Bonds and Guaranteed Securities

    For income bonds and guaranteed securities, carefully understand payment conditions and the legal strength of any guarantee.

    • Interest payments on an income bond may be contingent on earnings or specified conditions.
    • Verify the credit strength of the entity providing the guarantee.
    • Review the wording, scope, and enforceability of the guarantee.
    Study topic 20 of 30

    Protective Covenants and Remedies

    Covenants may protect bondholders from certain issuer actions, but enforcement depends on the contractual terms.

    • Review debt limits, asset sales, dividend restrictions, and reporting obligations.
    • Understand default remedies and the role of the trustee.
    • The presence of a covenant does not make an investment risk-free.
    Study topic 21 of 30

    Junior Capital and Preferred-Stock Protection

    Capital cushions and contractual rights matter when assessing the protection of securities that rank below senior claims.

    • Analyse how much loss the junior capital can absorb.
    • Review preferred-stock protections and provisions for dividend arrears.
    • Understand the priority of each claim in the capital structure.
    Study topic 22 of 30

    Supervision of Investment Holdings

    After purchasing a security, continue monitoring the issuer's condition and the original investment thesis.

    • Track earnings, debt, covenant compliance, and credit events.
    • Do not react only to a price decline; reassess the underlying facts.
    • Review portfolio risk and position size when material deterioration occurs.
    Study topic 23 of 30

    Speculative features wale senior issues

    Some senior securities may include conversion, participation, or other speculative features; their risks differ from those of a plain bond.

    • Analyse the base contractual claim separately from speculative upside.
    • Review conversion terms, dilution, and issuer solvency.
    • Do not overlook principal risk in pursuit of an upside feature.
    Study topic 24 of 30

    Common stock: dividend factor

    The 1934 edition examines dividend history as an important factor in common-stock analysis.

    • Dividend history may provide evidence of stability, but it does not guarantee future payments.
    • Evaluate the payout against earnings, cash flow, and debt.
    • Do not equate dividend yield with total investment return.
    Study topic 25 of 30

    Common stock: earnings factor

    Sustainable earning power is central to common-stock valuation; do not treat temporarily peak earnings as permanent.

    • Review multi-year results in the context of the business cycle.
    • Normalise exceptional items and accounting changes.
    • Assess the quality and repeatability of earnings.
    Study topic 26 of 30

    Analysis of the Income Statement

    Detailed analysis of income-statement items can clarify actual operating performance and fixed obligations.

    • Examine the relationships among revenue, operating expenses, interest, taxes, and net income.
    • Analyse depreciation and unusual charges in context.
    • Do not overlook consolidated subsidiaries or minority interests.
    Study topic 27 of 30

    Earnings Quality and Normalisation

    Reported earnings may include temporary gains or losses and cyclical peaks; estimate normalised earnings for valuation.

    • Use several years of results, not just the best year.
    • Separate gains from asset sales and one-time credits from recurring earnings.
    • Test earning power under both recessionary and boom scenarios.
    Study topic 28 of 30

    Earnings Multipliers and Valuation

    The earnings multiplier (P/E) relates market price to per-share earnings, but an appropriate multiple depends on context.

    • First determine whether earnings are stable or cyclical.
    • Growth, debt, business quality, and the interest-rate environment all matter.
    • A low P/E is not automatically a bargain; it may also signal a collapse in earnings.
    Study topic 29 of 30

    Balance-Sheet Analysis

    A balance sheet provides a snapshot of assets, liabilities, and equity; asset quality and obligations require detailed examination.

    • Assess the recoverability and realisable value of cash, receivables, inventory, and fixed assets.
    • Identify current liabilities, long-term debt, and contingent claims.
    • Do not mistake book value for market value or liquidation value.
    Study topic 30 of 30

    Asset Values and the Liquidation Perspective

    Asset-value analysis requires examining the actual economic value behind reported book values and the potential costs of realisation.

    • Inventory may become obsolete, receivables may not be collected, and fixed assets may need to be sold at a discount.
    • Intangible assets and hidden liabilities can materially change an estimate.
    • Liquidation value is a scenario, not a guaranteed recovery.
    The 1934 edition is a historical work. Today's accounting standards, markets, regulations, and financial instruments may differ. Verify these concepts against current company filings and modern standards.
