Trading Volatility: Correlation, Term Structure and Skew

Colin Bennett

A 30-topic study guide based on Colin Bennett's volatility research and trading concepts, covering realized and implied volatility, options Greeks, the volatility surface, skew, term structure, correlation, volatility derivatives, and risk management.

    Key concept

    Historical volatility vs implied volatility

    Volatility measures variation in asset returns; it does not indicate direction.

    • Historical volatility (HV) is calculated from past realized returns; the result depends on the selected period and calculation method.
    • Implied volatility (IV) is inferred from option prices and reflects the market's forward-looking pricing of uncertainty; it is not a guaranteed forecast.
    • High IV does not mean the market must move up or down; it may simply mean options are pricing in greater expected movement or a higher risk premium.
    Key concept

    Options basics: calls, puts and premium

    A call gives its buyer the right to buy the underlying asset, while a put gives its buyer the right to sell, subject to the contract terms.

    • A call can provide bullish exposure and a put can provide bearish or hedging exposure, but profit depends not only on direction, but also on the entry premium, strike, expiry, and IV.
    • An option buyer's maximum loss is generally the premium paid; an option writer's risk can be substantial depending on the position structure.
    • Moneyness, time to expiry, liquidity, bid-ask spread, and volatility all affect option prices.
    Key concept

    Option Greeks and Pricing

    Option models estimate theoretical prices; actual market prices are also influenced by supply, demand, and model assumptions.

    • Delta: an estimate of an option's price sensitivity to a small move in the underlying asset.
    • Gamma: how quickly delta changes; gamma risk can increase for at-the-money options near expiry.
    • Vega measures sensitivity to changes in implied volatility; Theta represents time decay; Rho measures interest-rate sensitivity.
    • Greeks are estimates, not fixed guarantees; market conditions and model assumptions matter.
    Key concept

    Volatility Smile and Skew

    Options at different strike prices do not necessarily have the same implied volatility; this pattern is called a smile or skew.

    • In equity index markets, downside puts sometimes have higher IV, which may be linked to demand for crash protection and tail-risk pricing.
    • The pattern changes with the asset class, expiry, and market regime; do not assume the same shape in every market.
    • Do not interpret skew as a standalone bullish or bearish signal.
    Key concept

    Volatility term structure: contango and backwardation

    The term structure compares implied volatility across different expiries for the same underlying asset.

    • An upward-sloping term structure is generally called contango: longer-dated IV is higher than shorter-dated IV.
    • In backwardation, near-term IV is higher than longer-dated IV; event risk or market stress may be one reason.
    • These labels describe the shape of the curve; they do not guarantee future realized volatility.
    Key concept

    Correlation and diversification

    Correlation describes how two return series move together; the coefficient generally ranges from -1 to +1.

    • A coefficient of +1 indicates perfect positive co-movement, 0 indicates no linear relationship, and -1 indicates perfect negative co-movement.
    • Diversification can reduce index or portfolio volatility when holdings are not perfectly correlated; correlations can change during a crisis.
    • Index volatility may often be lower than the volatility of individual constituent stocks, but this is not the definition of correlation; it reflects the combined effects of weights, covariances, and diversification.
    Key concept

    Volatility strategies and risk

    These strategies combine volatility exposure, direction, time decay, and event risk in different ways.

    • A long straddle or strangle generally requires a substantial move; the timing of that move and an IV crush also affect the outcome.
    • Iron condors and credit spreads seek to collect premium in range-bound markets, but sharp moves and gap risk can cause losses.
    • Calculate position size, maximum loss, liquidity, expiry risk, and stress scenarios in advance.
    Study topic 8 of 30

    Realized volatility measurement

    Realized volatility measures the dispersion of historical returns; the estimate changes with sampling and annualization.

    • Daily and intraday returns can produce different estimates.
    • Clearly document the observation window and annualisation convention.
    • Outliers and market closures can distort results.
    Study topic 9 of 30

    Volatility clustering and regimes

    Periods of high volatility can be followed by further high volatility; volatility is not constant.

    • Regime changes are possible.
    • A past regime does not guarantee future conditions.
    • Review risk limits as market conditions change.
    Study topic 10 of 30

    Forecasting volatility

    Volatility forecasts can be built from historical data, implied prices, and statistical models.

    • Ensure that the forecast horizon matches its inputs.
    • Allow for model error and sudden events.
    • A forecast is a probability estimate, not a certainty.
    Study topic 11 of 30

    Volatility risk premium

    The difference between implied and subsequently realized volatility is studied in the context of the volatility risk premium.

    • IV can be higher than realized volatility, but this is not always the case.
    • The premium may compensate investors for bearing risk.
    • Short-volatility strategies do not provide risk-free income.
    Study topic 12 of 30

    Volatility surface

    The volatility surface provides a combined view of implied volatility across strikes and expiries.

    • Smile/skew and term structure represent separate dimensions.
    • Liquidity and the interpolation method affect the result.
    • The volatility surface shifts over time.
    Study topic 13 of 30

    Forward volatility

    Forward volatility is a market-based estimate of implied volatility for a future period.

    • Forward variance can be derived from total variance.
    • Expiry dates and day-count conventions matter.
    • This does not guarantee future realized volatility.
    Study topic 14 of 30

    Variance swaps

    A variance swap is a derivative that provides a cash settlement based on the difference between realized variance and the contract strike.

    • The payoff is linked to variance.
    • Check the notional amount, observation period, and settlement formula.
    • Variance swaps and volatility swaps have different payoff structures.
    Study topic 15 of 30

    Volatility swaps

    A volatility swap's payoff is linked to the difference between realized volatility and the agreed strike.

    • This is a different instrument from a variance swap.
    • Convexity adjustments can affect pricing.
    • Read the contract definitions carefully.
    Study topic 16 of 30

    VIX and volatility indices

    Indices such as the VIX estimate a standardized measure of near-term implied volatility from option prices.

    • The VIX is a 30-day measure derived from S&P 500 option prices.
    • The VIX index is not a directly investable asset.
    • Returns from VIX futures and exchange-traded products may differ from the index.
    Study topic 17 of 30

    Volatility futures and roll yield

    The volatility futures curve may differ from the spot index; roll effects are important for futures exposure.

    • Contango can create a negative roll effect.
    • Roll dynamics may reverse during backwardation.
    • The spot index and futures-product returns are not the same.
    Study topic 18 of 30

    Event risk and implied volatility

    Events such as earnings announcements and central-bank decisions are reflected as uncertainty in option prices.

    • Implied volatility may rise ahead of an event.
    • The actual move may be smaller or larger than the implied move.
    • Verify expiry dates and event timing.
    Study topic 19 of 30

    Volatility crush

    Implied volatility may decline sharply after an event; this is known as a volatility crush.

    • An option buyer can lose money even when the market direction is predicted correctly.
    • Event-related uncertainty may already be reflected in the option premium.
    • Analyse the entry price, implied volatility, and breakeven level together.
    Study topic 20 of 30

    Skew dynamics and tail risk

    Skew can reflect relative demand for protection at different strikes and the pricing of tail risk.

    • Put skew may be linked to demand for downside insurance.
    • Skew is not a standalone directional signal.
    • Crash protection can be expensive and sensitive to timing.
    Study topic 21 of 30

    Risk reversals and butterflies

    Risk reversals and butterflies are option combinations that create exposure to skew and smile.

    • A risk reversal compares the relative pricing of calls and puts.
    • A butterfly provides exposure to curvature across selected strikes.
    • Keep expiry and delta conventions clear.
    Study topic 22 of 30

    Calendar spreads and term-structure trades

    Calendar spreads create relative time-volatility exposure by buying and selling options with different expiries.

    • The difference between near-term and long-term implied volatility matters.
    • Time decay and event dates are important.
    • Check maximum risk, margin requirements, and exercise terms.
    Study topic 23 of 30

    Dispersion trading

    Dispersion strategies target relative pricing differences between index volatility and constituent volatility.

    • Index and single-stock option exposures are different.
    • Correlation and constituent weights affect the result.
    • Hedging and transaction costs can be significant.
    Study topic 24 of 30

    Correlation trading

    Correlation exposure is related to the joint movement of multiple assets and their covariance structure.

    • Correlation is not static.
    • Correlation can rise during periods of market stress.
    • Basket weights and composition affect the measurement.
    Study topic 25 of 30

    Covariance and portfolio volatility

    Portfolio volatility depends on asset volatilities, covariance, and portfolio weights.

    • Portfolio variance includes covariance terms.
    • Diversification can reduce risk, but it cannot eliminate it.
    • Assess concentration and liquidity stress separately.
    Study topic 26 of 30

    Hedging volatility exposure

    Options and related instruments can hedge volatility exposure, but no hedge is perfect.

    • A delta hedge reduces price sensitivity, not volatility risk.
    • Monitor vega and gamma separately.
    • Include basis risk, rebalancing, and transaction costs.
    Study topic 27 of 30

    Higher-order Greeks

    Higher-order Greeks describe nonlinear sensitivities in option values.

    • Vanna measures the interaction between delta and volatility.
    • Volga, also known as vomma, describes the sensitivity of vega to volatility.
    • Charm captures changes in delta over time.
    Study topic 28 of 30

    Model risk and assumptions

    Option models use simplifying assumptions; real markets can experience jumps and liquidity gaps.

    • Verify model inputs and calibration sources.
    • Compare stress scenarios and alternative models.
    • A model output is not an executable market quote.
    Study topic 29 of 30

    Liquidity, bid-ask and execution

    There may be a meaningful difference between theoretical value and the actual executable price.

    • Check the bid-ask spread, open interest, and market depth.
    • Large orders can create slippage.
    • Consider exit liquidity before entering a position.
    Study topic 30 of 30

    Portfolio construction and stress testing

    Volatility positions should be sized within the portfolio's overall risk budget.

    • Stress-test spot gaps, implied-volatility shifts, and correlation jumps.
    • Estimate margin calls and liquidity needs.
    • Monitor maximum loss and counterparty exposure.
    Options and volatility strategies are complex. Short-volatility positions can incur very large and, in some cases, theoretically unlimited losses. These notes are not trading recommendations; do not trade options without paper trading and appropriate risk controls.
